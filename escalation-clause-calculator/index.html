How escalation clauses are calculated

An escalation clause says the buyer will beat any competing offer by a set amount, up to a maximum. So an offer of $455,000 that escalates by $2,000 up to $480,000 becomes $474,000 if the best competing offer is $472,000.

The calculator uses three rules:

  1. One escalating offer reaches the lower of (the highest other offer + its increment) and its cap. It never drops below its own starting price.
  2. Two or more escalating offers push against each other until all but one hit their caps. The offer with the highest cap wins, at the lower of (the next highest possible price + its increment) and its cap.
  3. Equal caps have no clear winner. The result depends on the clause wording and timing, and the calculator flags it.

Price or price after credits?

If a competing offer asks for $8,000 in seller credits, some escalation clauses count its full price and some count the price after credits. The two readings can give different results, so switch the option above and compare. Check the clause wording to see which one applies.

How the seller's net is estimated

Net = final price − seller credits − commission (a percent of the final price) − mortgage payoff − other selling costs. It is an estimate. Title, transfer taxes and prorations vary, so add them to other costs for a closer number.

Highest price is not the same as best offer

An escalated price only matters if the deal closes. A financed offer far above list price can face an appraisal gap, and a home sale contingency can fall through. Weigh each offer's financing, contingencies and timing alongside the net.

This calculator is for estimates. It is not legal or tax advice. Follow your brokerage policy and local rules on disclosing offers, since escalation clauses usually require proof of the competing offer.
